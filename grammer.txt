\chapter*{Preface}
English grammar is the system of rules and patterns that allows speakers and writers to communicate clearly, accurately, and effectively. This book presents the main structures of English grammar in a systematic and learner-friendly way. It is suitable for students, teachers, self-learners, and writers who want a dependable reference.

This book is divided into chapters covering parts of speech, sentence structure, tense and aspect, agreement, punctuation, clauses, common errors, and style. Each section includes definitions, rules, examples, and practical notes.

\chapter*{How to Use This Book}
You may read this book from beginning to end or use it as a reference. Beginners may start with the chapters on parts of speech and sentence structure. Intermediate learners may focus on tense, clauses, and modifiers. Advanced learners may review conditionals, voice, reported speech, and style.

\chapter{What Is Grammar?}
Grammar is the framework of a language. It governs how words change form, how they combine into phrases and clauses, and how meaning is expressed.

\section{Why Grammar Matters}
Grammar matters because it helps speakers and writers:
\begin{itemize}
    \item express meaning clearly,
    \item avoid ambiguity,
    \item write correctly and persuasively,
    \item understand formal and informal registers,
    \item communicate effectively in academic and professional settings.
\end{itemize}

\section{The Main Branches of Grammar}
English grammar may be studied under several headings:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \term{Morphology}: the structure of words.
    \item \term{Syntax}: the arrangement of words into sentences.
    \item \term{Semantics}: meaning.
    \item \term{Punctuation and mechanics}: written conventions.
    \item \term{Usage}: accepted patterns in real communication.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{rulebox}
Grammar describes both \emph{rules} and \emph{patterns of usage}. Some rules are fixed; others depend on context, style, and variety of English.
\end{rulebox}

\chapter{Parts of Speech}
A \term{part of speech} is a category of words defined by function and form.

\section{Overview}
The major parts of speech in English are:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item nouns
    \item pronouns
    \item verbs
    \item adjectives
    \item adverbs
    \item prepositions
    \item conjunctions
    \item interjections
    \item determiners (often treated separately in modern grammar)
\end{enumerate}

\section{Nouns}
A \term{noun} names a person, place, thing, idea, or quality.

\subsection{Types of Nouns}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Common nouns}: city, teacher, book
    \item \textbf{Proper nouns}: India, Ravi, London
    \item \textbf{Concrete nouns}: apple, table, dog
    \item \textbf{Abstract nouns}: honesty, freedom, wisdom
    \item \textbf{Count nouns}: chair/chairs, idea/ideas
    \item \textbf{Non-count nouns}: water, furniture, advice
    \item \textbf{Collective nouns}: team, committee, family
\end{itemize}

\example{The \textbf{committee} met after lunch.}

\subsection{Count and Non-count Nouns}
Count nouns can be counted individually. Non-count nouns usually refer to substances, concepts, or masses.

\begin{longtable}{p{0.45\textwidth}p{0.45\textwidth}}
\toprule
\textbf{Count Nouns} & \textbf{Non-count Nouns} \\
\midrule
book, pen, idea, student & milk, rice, information, furniture \\
many books & much milk \\
a few students & a little rice \\
\bottomrule
\end{longtable}

\begin{warningbox}
Do not say \emph{an advice} or \emph{informations} in standard English. Use \emph{a piece of advice} and \emph{information}.
\end{warningbox}

\section{Pronouns}
A \term{pronoun} replaces a noun or noun phrase.

\subsection{Types of Pronouns}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Personal: I, you, he, she, it, we, they
    \item Object: me, him, her, us, them
    \item Possessive: mine, yours, his, hers, ours, theirs
    \item Reflexive: myself, yourself, herself
    \item Demonstrative: this, that, these, those
    \item Relative: who, whom, whose, which, that
    \item Interrogative: who, what, which
    \item Indefinite: someone, anyone, none, several, many
\end{itemize}

\section{Verbs}
A \term{verb} expresses action, occurrence, or state.

\subsection{Main Types of Verbs}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Action verbs}: run, write, sing
    \item \textbf{Linking verbs}: be, seem, become
    \item \textbf{Auxiliary verbs}: be, have, do
    \item \textbf{Modal verbs}: can, may, must, should, will
\end{itemize}

\section{Adjectives}
An \term{adjective} describes or modifies a noun or pronoun.

\example{a \textbf{beautiful} house, an \textbf{interesting} lesson}

\section{Adverbs}
An \term{adverb} modifies a verb, adjective, another adverb, or sometimes a whole sentence.

\example{She speaks \textbf{clearly}.}

\section{Prepositions}
A \term{preposition} shows the relationship between a noun (or pronoun) and another element in the sentence.

\example{on the table, in the room, after lunch}

\section{Conjunctions}
A \term{conjunction} connects words, phrases, or clauses.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Coordinating: and, but, or, nor, for, so, yet
    \item Subordinating: because, although, if, when, since
    \item Correlative: either...or, neither...nor, not only...but also
\end{itemize}

\section{Interjections}
An \term{interjection} expresses sudden feeling or emotion.

\example{\textbf{Oh!} That was unexpected.}

\section{Determiners}
A \term{determiner} introduces a noun and gives information such as quantity, definiteness, or possession.

\example{the book, a student, my pen, some water, each child}

\chapter{Nouns and Their Forms}

\section{Singular and Plural}
Most nouns form the plural by adding \texttt{-s} or \texttt{-es}.

\begin{itemize}
    \item book $\rightarrow$ books
    \item class $\rightarrow$ classes
    \item box $\rightarrow$ boxes
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Irregular Plurals}
\begin{itemize}
    \item man $\rightarrow$ men
    \item woman $\rightarrow$ women
    \item child $\rightarrow$ children
    \item foot $\rightarrow$ feet
    \item tooth $\rightarrow$ teeth
    \item mouse $\rightarrow$ mice
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Nouns with Same Singular and Plural Form}
\begin{itemize}
    \item sheep
    \item deer
    \item series
    \item species
\end{itemize}

\section{Possessive Forms}
Possession is usually shown with an apostrophe plus \texttt{s}.

\begin{itemize}
    \item the girl's book
    \item Ravi's car
    \item the teachers' room
\end{itemize}

\begin{rulebox}
For singular nouns, add \texttt{'s}. For regular plural nouns ending in \texttt{s}, add only an apostrophe.
\end{rulebox}

\section{Compound Nouns}
Compound nouns may be written as one word, two words, or hyphenated.

\begin{itemize}
    \item toothpaste
    \item bus stop
    \item mother-in-law
\end{itemize}

The plural usually applies to the principal word:
\begin{itemize}
    \item mothers-in-law
    \item passers-by
\end{itemize}

\chapter{Articles and Determiners}

\section{Articles}
English has two types of articles:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \term{Definite article}: the
    \item \term{Indefinite articles}: a, an
\end{itemize}

\section{Using \textit{a} and \textit{an}}
Use \textit{a} before a consonant sound and \textit{an} before a vowel sound.

\begin{itemize}
    \item a book
    \item a university (\emph{yoo} sound)
    \item an apple
    \item an hour (silent \textit{h})
\end{itemize}

\section{Using \textit{the}}
Use \textit{the} when the noun is specific or already known.

\example{I saw a dog. The dog was barking loudly.}

Use \textit{the} with:
\begin{itemize}
    \item unique things: the sun, the earth
    \item superlatives: the best student
    \item rivers, seas, oceans: the Nile, the Arabian Sea
    \item mountain ranges: the Himalayas
    \item newspapers and some institutions: the Times, the bank
\end{itemize}

\section{Zero Article}
No article is used in some cases:
\begin{itemize}
    \item before plural or non-count nouns used generally: Books are useful. Water is essential.
    \item before names of most countries, people, streets, and languages
    \item before meals in a general sense: We had breakfast.
\end{itemize}

\section{Other Determiners}
\subsection{Demonstratives}
this, that, these, those

\subsection{Quantifiers}
some, any, much, many, little, few, several, enough, all, both, each, every, either, neither

\subsection{Possessives}
my, your, his, her, its, our, their

\begin{warningbox}
Do not combine two central determiners incorrectly. In standard English, \emph{the my book} is wrong. Say \emph{my book} or \emph{the book}.
\end{warningbox}

\chapter{Pronouns}

\section{Personal Pronouns}
\begin{longtable}{llll}
\toprule
\textbf{Person} & \textbf{Subject} & \textbf{Object} & \textbf{Possessive} \\
\midrule
1st singular & I & me & mine \\
2nd singular/plural & you & you & yours \\
3rd singular masculine & he & him & his \\
3rd singular feminine & she & her & hers \\
3rd singular neuter & it & it & its \\
1st plural & we & us & ours \\
3rd plural & they & them & theirs \\
\bottomrule
\end{longtable}

\section{Reflexive and Intensive Pronouns}
myself, yourself, himself, herself, itself, ourselves, yourselves, themselves

\example{She taught herself French.}

\section{Relative Pronouns}
Relative pronouns introduce relative clauses.

\begin{itemize}
    \item who: for people
    \item whom: object form for people
    \item whose: possession
    \item which: for things and animals
    \item that: for people or things in restrictive clauses
\end{itemize}

\example{The student who won the prize is my friend.}

\section{Indefinite Pronouns}
Examples include someone, anyone, everyone, nobody, several, many, much, all, none, either, neither.

\begin{rulebox}
Some indefinite pronouns are singular in standard formal English: everyone, each, either, neither, somebody, anyone.
\end{rulebox}

\example{Everyone \textbf{is} ready.}

\chapter{Verbs: Forms and Functions}

\section{Principal Parts of Verbs}
Most English verbs have these principal forms:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item base form: write
    \item third-person singular present: writes
    \item past tense: wrote
    \item past participle: written
    \item present participle/gerund: writing
\end{enumerate}

\section{Regular and Irregular Verbs}
Regular verbs form the past tense with \texttt{-ed}; irregular verbs do not.

\begin{itemize}
    \item work -- worked -- worked
    \item go -- went -- gone
    \item sing -- sang -- sung
\end{itemize}

\section{Transitive and Intransitive Verbs}
A \term{transitive verb} takes a direct object. An \term{intransitive verb} does not.

\example{She opened the door.} (transitive) \\
\example{He slept deeply.} (intransitive)

\section{Finite and Non-finite Verbs}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Finite verbs} show tense and agree with the subject.
    \item \textbf{Non-finite verbs} do not show tense independently.
\end{itemize}

Non-finite forms include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item infinitives: to write
    \item gerunds: writing
    \item participles: written, writing
\end{itemize}

\section{Auxiliary Verbs}
The main auxiliary verbs are \textit{be}, \textit{have}, and \textit{do}.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{be}: is writing, was taken
    \item \textbf{have}: has finished
    \item \textbf{do}: do you know?
\end{itemize}

\section{Modal Verbs}
Modal verbs express ability, possibility, permission, obligation, prediction, or advice.

\begin{itemize}
    \item can / could
    \item may / might
    \item shall / should
    \item will / would
    \item must
\end{itemize}

\begin{rulebox}
Modals are followed by the base form of the verb: \emph{She can swim}, not \emph{She can swims}.
\end{rulebox}

\chapter{Tense and Aspect}

\section{Introduction}
English tense shows time, while aspect shows how an action unfolds: whether it is simple, ongoing, completed, or completed up to a point.

\section{The Twelve Common Tense-Aspect Forms}

\subsection{Present Simple}
Form: subject + base verb / \texttt{-s} form for third person singular

Uses:
\begin{itemize}
    \item habits: She walks every morning.
    \item general truths: Water boils at 100\textdegree C.
    \item schedules: The train leaves at 6:00.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Present Continuous}
Form: am/is/are + present participle

Uses:
\begin{itemize}
    \item actions happening now: I am reading.
    \item temporary situations: She is staying with her aunt.
    \item changing situations: Prices are rising.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Present Perfect}
Form: has/have + past participle

Uses:
\begin{itemize}
    \item past action with present relevance: I have finished my work.
    \item life experience: He has visited Japan.
    \item unfinished time period: We have seen her this week.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Present Perfect Continuous}
Form: has/have been + present participle

Uses:
\begin{itemize}
    \item duration from past to present: I have been studying for two hours.
    \item recently continuing activity: It has been raining.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Past Simple}
Form: verb in past form

Uses:
\begin{itemize}
    \item completed action in the past: She left yesterday.
    \item sequence of events: He opened the door, entered the room, and sat down.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Past Continuous}
Form: was/were + present participle

Uses:
\begin{itemize}
    \item action in progress at a past time: I was sleeping at 10 p.m.
    \item background action: While I was cooking, the phone rang.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Past Perfect}
Form: had + past participle

Uses:
\begin{itemize}
    \item earlier past action before another past action: She had left before I arrived.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Past Perfect Continuous}
Form: had been + present participle

Uses:
\begin{itemize}
    \item duration before a point in the past: They had been waiting for an hour when the bus came.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Future Simple}
Form: will + base verb

Uses:
\begin{itemize}
    \item prediction: It will rain tomorrow.
    \item spontaneous decision: I will help you.
    \item promise: I will call you tonight.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Future Continuous}
Form: will be + present participle

Uses:
\begin{itemize}
    \item action in progress at a future time: This time tomorrow, we will be flying.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Future Perfect}
Form: will have + past participle

Uses:
\begin{itemize}
    \item completed action before a future point: By next week, I will have finished the report.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Future Perfect Continuous}
Form: will have been + present participle

Uses:
\begin{itemize}
    \item duration up to a future point: By June, she will have been working here for ten years.
\end{itemize}

\section{Tense Summary Table}
\begin{longtable}{p{0.25\textwidth}p{0.25\textwidth}p{0.4\textwidth}}
\toprule
\textbf{Tense} & \textbf{Form} & \textbf{Example} \\
\midrule
Present Simple & write / writes & She writes daily. \\
Present Continuous & is writing & She is writing now. \\
Present Perfect & has written & She has written the letter. \\
Present Perfect Continuous & has been writing & She has been writing for an hour. \\
Past Simple & wrote & She wrote yesterday. \\
Past Continuous & was writing & She was writing at noon. \\
Past Perfect & had written & She had written before lunch. \\
Past Perfect Continuous & had been writing & She had been writing for an hour. \\
Future Simple & will write & She will write tomorrow. \\
Future Continuous & will be writing & She will be writing at noon. \\
Future Perfect & will have written & She will have written by noon. \\
Future Perfect Continuous & will have been writing & She will have been writing for an hour. \\
\bottomrule
\end{longtable}

\chapter{Subject--Verb Agreement}

\section{Basic Rule}
A singular subject takes a singular verb; a plural subject takes a plural verb.

\begin{itemize}
    \item The boy runs.
    \item The boys run.
\end{itemize}

\section{Common Agreement Patterns}
\subsection{Words Between Subject and Verb}
Ignore prepositional phrases between subject and verb.

\example{The box of chocolates \textbf{is} on the table.}

\subsection{Compound Subjects}
Subjects joined by \textit{and} usually take a plural verb.

\example{Ravi and Neha \textbf{are} here.}

If the compound subject refers to one unit or idea, singular agreement may occur.

\example{Bread and butter \textbf{is} my breakfast.}

\subsection{Either/Neither}
With \textit{either...or} and \textit{neither...nor}, the verb often agrees with the nearer subject.

\example{Either the teachers or the principal \textbf{is} attending.}

\subsection{Collective Nouns}
Collective nouns may take singular or plural verbs depending on whether the group is seen as a unit or as individuals.

\example{The team \textbf{is} winning.} \\
\example{The team \textbf{are} arguing among themselves.}

\subsection{Indefinite Pronouns}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Singular: everyone, someone, nobody, each, either, neither
    \item Plural: few, many, several
    \item Variable: all, some, none, most
\end{itemize}

\example{Each of the students \textbf{has} submitted the assignment.}

\chapter{Adjectives and Adverbs}

\section{Adjectives}
Adjectives describe nouns or pronouns.

\subsection{Types of Adjectives}
\begin{itemize}
    \item descriptive: tall, honest, red
    \item quantitative: some, many, several
    \item demonstrative: this, those
    \item possessive: my, your
    \item interrogative: which, what, whose
\end{itemize}

\section{Order of Adjectives}
When multiple adjectives precede a noun, they often follow a common order:
\begin{center}
opinion -- size -- age -- shape -- color -- origin -- material -- purpose -- noun
\end{center}

\example{a lovely small old round brown Italian wooden coffee table}

\section{Comparison of Adjectives}
\subsection{Degrees of Comparison}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Positive: tall
    \item Comparative: taller
    \item Superlative: tallest
\end{itemize}

Longer adjectives usually take \textit{more} and \textit{most}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item more beautiful
    \item most beautiful
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Irregular Comparison}
\begin{itemize}
    \item good -- better -- best
    \item bad -- worse -- worst
    \item little -- less -- least
    \item many/much -- more -- most
\end{itemize}

\section{Adverbs}
Adverbs modify verbs, adjectives, or other adverbs.

\subsection{Types of Adverbs}
\begin{itemize}
    \item manner: slowly, carefully
    \item time: now, yesterday, soon
    \item place: here, there, everywhere
    \item frequency: always, often, sometimes
    \item degree: very, quite, almost
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Adjective vs.\ Adverb}
\begin{itemize}
    \item She is a \textbf{careful} driver. (adjective)
    \item She drives \textbf{carefully}. (adverb)
\end{itemize}

\begin{warningbox}
Do not confuse \emph{good} and \emph{well}. \emph{Good} is usually an adjective; \emph{well} is usually an adverb.
\end{warningbox}

\chapter{Phrases and Clauses}

\section{Phrase}
A \term{phrase} is a group of related words without a finite verb acting as the main verb.

Types of phrases include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item noun phrase
    \item verb phrase
    \item adjective phrase
    \item adverb phrase
    \item prepositional phrase
\end{itemize}

\example{the tall boy in the blue shirt}

\section{Clause}
A \term{clause} is a group of words containing a subject and a predicate.

\subsection{Independent Clause}
An independent clause can stand alone as a sentence.

\example{The rain stopped.}

\subsection{Dependent Clause}
A dependent clause cannot stand alone.

\example{because the rain stopped}

\section{Types of Dependent Clauses}
\begin{itemize}
    \item noun clauses
    \item adjective (relative) clauses
    \item adverb clauses
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Noun Clauses}
A noun clause functions as a noun.

\example{What she said was true.}

\subsection{Adjective Clauses}
An adjective clause modifies a noun.

\example{The book that I borrowed is excellent.}

\subsection{Adverb Clauses}
An adverb clause modifies a verb, adjective, or adverb.

\example{I left because I was tired.}

\chapter{Sentence Structure}

\section{The Sentence}
A sentence expresses a complete thought and usually contains a subject and a predicate.

\section{Sentence Elements}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Subject
    \item Predicate
    \item Object
    \item Complement
    \item Modifier
\end{itemize}

\section{Kinds of Sentences by Structure}
\subsection{Simple Sentence}
One independent clause.

\example{The child laughed.}

\subsection{Compound Sentence}
Two or more independent clauses joined by a coordinator or semicolon.

\example{The child laughed, and the mother smiled.}

\subsection{Complex Sentence}
One independent clause and at least one dependent clause.

\example{The child laughed because the clown smiled.}

\subsection{Compound-Complex Sentence}
At least two independent clauses and at least one dependent clause.

\example{The child laughed because the clown smiled, and the audience applauded.}

\section{Kinds of Sentences by Purpose}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Declarative: She is ready.
    \item Interrogative: Is she ready?
    \item Imperative: Please sit down.
    \item Exclamatory: What a beautiful view!
\end{itemize}

\chapter{Negation, Questions, and Commands}

\section{Negation}
Negation is commonly formed with \textit{not}.

\begin{itemize}
    \item She is not ready.
    \item They do not agree.
    \item He cannot swim.
\end{itemize}

\section{Do-Support}
English often uses \textit{do}, \textit{does}, or \textit{did} to form negatives and questions in the simple present and simple past.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Do you know the answer?
    \item She does not like coffee.
    \item Did they arrive on time?
\end{itemize}

\section{Yes/No Questions}
These usually begin with an auxiliary verb or modal.

\example{Are you ready?}

\section{Wh-Questions}
These begin with question words such as who, what, where, when, why, and how.

\example{Where do you live?}

\section{Tag Questions}
A tag question is added to a statement to seek confirmation.

\example{You are coming, aren't you?}

\section{Imperatives}
Imperatives give commands, requests, advice, or instructions.

\example{Open the window.} \\
\example{Please be careful.}

\chapter{Active and Passive Voice}

\section{Active Voice}
In the active voice, the subject performs the action.

\example{The teacher explained the lesson.}

\section{Passive Voice}
In the passive voice, the subject receives the action.

\example{The lesson was explained by the teacher.}

\section{Form of the Passive}
Passive voice is formed with:
\begin{center}
appropriate form of \textit{be} + past participle
\end{center}

\section{When to Use the Passive}
Use the passive when:
\begin{itemize}
    \item the doer is unknown,
    \item the doer is unimportant,
    \item the focus should be on the action or result,
    \item a formal or impersonal tone is desired.
\end{itemize}

\section{Common Passive Patterns}
\begin{itemize}
    \item The work is finished.
    \item The report was submitted yesterday.
    \item The road has been repaired.
    \item The meeting will be held tomorrow.
\end{itemize}

\begin{warningbox}
Not all active sentences should be changed into passive ones. Overuse of passive voice can make writing weak or unclear.
\end{warningbox}

\chapter{Mood, Modality, and Conditionals}

\section{Mood}
Mood shows the speaker's attitude toward the action.

\subsection{Indicative Mood}
Used for facts and statements.

\example{She lives in Pune.}

\subsection{Imperative Mood}
Used for commands and requests.

\example{Please close the door.}

\subsection{Subjunctive Mood}
Used in certain formal expressions, suggestions, and unreal situations.

\example{I suggest that he \textbf{be} informed immediately.}

\section{Modality}
Modality expresses necessity, possibility, probability, permission, and ability.

\begin{itemize}
    \item can: ability, informal permission
    \item may: permission, possibility
    \item must: strong obligation
    \item should: advice, expectation
    \item might: weaker possibility
    \item would: polite request, hypothetical meaning
\end{itemize}

\section{Conditionals}
\subsection{Zero Conditional}
General truth:
\example{If you heat ice, it melts.}

\subsection{First Conditional}
Real future possibility:
\example{If it rains, we will stay inside.}

\subsection{Second Conditional}
Unreal or unlikely present/future:
\example{If I had more time, I would learn Arabic.}

\subsection{Third Conditional}
Unreal past:
\example{If she had studied, she would have passed.}

\subsection{Mixed Conditional}
Mixes time reference:
\example{If I had taken that job, I would be living abroad now.}

% ============================================================
\chapter{Non-finite Verb Forms: Infinitives, Gerunds, and Participles}

\section{Infinitives}
The infinitive is usually the base verb preceded by \textit{to}.

\example{to read, to write, to speak}

Infinitives may function as nouns, adjectives, or adverbs.

\example{To read is useful.} \\
\example{I need a book to read.} \\
\example{She studied to succeed.}

\section{Gerunds}
A gerund is the \texttt{-ing} form used as a noun.

\example{Swimming is good exercise.}

\section{Participles}
Participles can function as adjectives or as part of verb phrases.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Present participle: smiling, running
    \item Past participle: broken, written, spoken
\end{itemize}

\section{Gerund vs.\ Infinitive}
Some verbs are followed by gerunds, some by infinitives, and some by either.

\subsection{Common Verbs Followed by Gerunds}
enjoy, avoid, finish, admit, consider, suggest

\example{She enjoys reading.}

\subsection{Common Verbs Followed by Infinitives}
want, decide, hope, plan, promise, refuse

\example{They decided to leave.}

\subsection{Verbs That May Take Either}
begin, start, continue, like, love, prefer

\example{He likes reading. / He likes to read.}

\chapter{Direct and Reported Speech}

\section{Direct Speech}
Direct speech reproduces the exact words spoken.

\example{She said, ``I am tired.''}

\section{Reported Speech}
Reported speech conveys the meaning without quoting exact words.

\example{She said that she was tired.}

\section{Backshift of Tenses}
When the reporting verb is in the past, the tense in the reported clause often shifts back.

\begin{longtable}{p{0.4\textwidth}p{0.4\textwidth}}
\toprule
\textbf{Direct Speech} & \textbf{Reported Speech} \\
\midrule
am/is/are & was/were \\
have/has & had \\
will & would \\
can & could \\
may & might \\
\bottomrule
\end{longtable}

\section{Changes in Time and Place Words}
\begin{itemize}
    \item now $\rightarrow$ then
    \item today $\rightarrow$ that day
    \item tomorrow $\rightarrow$ the next day
    \item yesterday $\rightarrow$ the previous day
    \item here $\rightarrow$ there
\end{itemize}

\section{Reporting Questions and Commands}
\begin{itemize}
    \item He asked where I lived.
    \item She told me to wait.
    \item They asked whether I was ready.
\end{itemize}

\chapter{Punctuation and Capitalization}

\section{Capitalization}
Use capital letters:
\begin{itemize}
    \item at the beginning of a sentence,
    \item for proper nouns,
    \item for days, months, and holidays,
    \item for the pronoun \textit{I},
    \item in titles according to style conventions.
\end{itemize}

\section{The Full Stop}
A full stop ends a declarative sentence.

\example{The meeting begins at nine.}

\section{The Comma}
Use commas:
\begin{itemize}
    \item in lists,
    \item after introductory elements,
    \item before coordinating conjunctions joining independent clauses,
    \item around non-essential information.
\end{itemize}

\example{After the meeting, we went home.}

\section{The Semicolon}
Use a semicolon to join closely related independent clauses.

\example{The sky was clear; the stars were bright.}

\section{The Colon}
Use a colon before lists, explanations, or formal statements.

\example{Bring the following items: a notebook, a pen, and your ID card.}

\section{Apostrophes}
Use apostrophes for possession and contractions.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Priya's bag
    \item don't, can't, it's
\end{itemize}

\begin{warningbox}
Do not confuse \emph{its} (possessive) with \emph{it's} (it is / it has).
\end{warningbox}

\section{Quotation Marks}
Use quotation marks for direct speech and quoted material.

\section{Question Marks and Exclamation Marks}
Use a question mark after a direct question and an exclamation mark for strong emotion or emphasis.

\chapter{Common Errors in English Grammar}

\section{Sentence Fragments}
A sentence fragment is an incomplete sentence.

\textbf{Incorrect:} Because I was tired. \\
\textbf{Correct:} I went to bed early because I was tired.

\section{Run-on Sentences}
A run-on sentence joins independent clauses incorrectly.

\textbf{Incorrect:} I finished my work I went home. \\
\textbf{Correct:} I finished my work, and I went home. \\
\textbf{Correct:} I finished my work; I went home.

\section{Comma Splices}
A comma splice uses only a comma to join independent clauses.

\textbf{Incorrect:} She was hungry, she made a sandwich. \\
\textbf{Correct:} She was hungry, so she made a sandwich.

\section{Misplaced Modifiers}
A misplaced modifier is placed too far from the word it describes.

\textbf{Incorrect:} She nearly drove the children to school every day. \\
\textbf{Correct:} She drove the children to school nearly every day.

\section{Dangling Modifiers}
A dangling modifier lacks a clear subject.

\textbf{Incorrect:} Walking down the street, the trees looked beautiful. \\
\textbf{Correct:} Walking down the street, I thought the trees looked beautiful.

\section{Pronoun Reference Errors}
Pronouns must refer clearly to nouns.

\textbf{Unclear:} When Ravi spoke to Aman, he was upset. \\
\textbf{Clearer:} Ravi was upset when he spoke to Aman.

\section{Parallelism Errors}
Items in a list or comparison should have the same grammatical form.

\textbf{Incorrect:} She likes reading, to swim, and cycling. \\
\textbf{Correct:} She likes reading, swimming, and cycling.

\section{Double Negatives}
Double negatives are often non-standard in formal English.

\textbf{Incorrect:} I don't know nothing about it. \\
\textbf{Correct:} I don't know anything about it.

\section{Confusing Word Pairs}
\begin{itemize}
    \item affect / effect
    \item fewer / less
    \item lie / lay
    \item who / whom
    \item your / you're
    \item their / there / they're
\end{itemize}

\chapter{Style, Clarity, and Formal Usage}

\section{Clarity}
Good grammar supports clarity. Prefer precise words and well-structured sentences.

\section{Conciseness}
Avoid unnecessary repetition.

\textbf{Wordy:} At this point in time, we are in need of assistance. \\
\textbf{Concise:} We need assistance now.

\section{Formal and Informal English}
English varies by context. Formal English is usually appropriate for academic, legal, and professional writing. Informal English is common in conversation and personal communication.

\section{Consistency}
Maintain consistency in:
\begin{itemize}
    \item tense
    \item point of view
    \item spelling variety (British or American)
    \item punctuation style
    \item capitalization
\end{itemize}

\section{Inclusive and Respectful Language}
Use language that is respectful, clear, and appropriate to the context.

\section{Active vs.\ Passive Style}
In general, prefer active voice for clarity unless the passive better suits the purpose.

\chapter{A Short Guide to Sentence Analysis}

\section{Finding the Subject}
Ask: who or what is the sentence about?

\example{The old bridge across the river collapsed.}

Subject: \textbf{bridge}

\section{Finding the Verb}
Ask: what is happening, or what state is being described?

Verb: \textbf{collapsed}

\section{Finding the Object}
If the verb acts on something, that something may be the object.

\example{The manager approved the proposal.}

Object: \textbf{proposal}

\section{Finding Complements}
Complements complete the meaning of a linking verb.

\example{She became a doctor.}

Complement: \textbf{a doctor}

\chapter{Conclusion}
English grammar is best learned through both study and use. Rules become meaningful when they are applied in reading, writing, listening, and speaking. Mastery comes gradually through observation, practice, correction, and revision.

A strong command of grammar supports confidence, precision, and elegance in communication. Use this book as a reference, a study guide, and a tool for continual improvement.

\chapter*{Glossary}
\defn{Adjective}{A word that modifies a noun or pronoun.}
\defn{Adverb}{A word that modifies a verb, adjective, adverb, or whole sentence.}
\defn{Clause}{A group of words containing a subject and a verb.}
\defn{Conjunction}{A word that joins words, phrases, or clauses.}
\defn{Determiner}{A word that introduces a noun and specifies it.}
\defn{Finite Verb}{A verb form that shows tense and agrees with the subject.}
\defn{Gerund}{An \texttt{-ing} form used as a noun.}
\defn{Infinitive}{Usually the base form of a verb preceded by \textit{to}.}
\defn{Modifier}{A word, phrase, or clause that describes another element.}
\defn{Noun}{A word naming a person, place, thing, or idea.}
\defn{Participle}{A verb form used in verb phrases or as an adjective.}
\defn{Phrase}{A group of related words without a complete finite verb structure.}
\defn{Predicate}{The part of the sentence that says something about the subject.}
\defn{Preposition}{A word showing relation between a noun/pronoun and another word.}
\defn{Pronoun}{A word used in place of a noun.}
\defn{Subject}{The person, thing, or idea that the sentence is about.}
\defn{Verb}{A word expressing action, occurrence, or state.}
